The BitPhilology Project

Elena Spadini, Elena Barchielli (Universität Bern)

Contemporary literary works are increasingly created in digital format, yet philology, archival science and scholarly publishing remain tied to analogue models. The Bit Philology project addresses the challenges of born-digital literary archives, treating them not merely as objects to be preserved but as primary philological sources that require new descriptive and interpretative models.

Through the study of Italian- and French-language literary archives in Switzerland, Bit Philology analyses files held on digital media using digital forensics methods and tools. It aims to develop a metadata schema specific to born-digital literary archives, grounded in emerging standards and ontologies, and to experiment with new forms of digital editing, such as a “text-as-bits” approach that decomposes word processor bitstreams to identify hidden textual witnesses.

The project also applies computational methods, including topic modeling, phylogenetic analysis and automated collation, to reconstruct genetic dossiers by tracing lexical patterns across drafts. By integrating philological, archival and digital forensics approaches, it proposes theoretical and operational tools for the description, editing and promotion of digital literary sources, thereby contributing to the preservation of cultural memory in the digital age.

Elena Spadini is assistant professor of digital humanities at the University of Bern, where she directs the Bit Philology project. She has been working as a researcher and data scientist with a focus on digital philology across Europe. Her research interests include the long history of text technologies, tool criticism, and modeling in the web of data.

Elena Barchielli is a PhD student at the University of Bern. She holds a MA in Digital and Public Humanities from the University of Venice and has been working as a researcher at the University of Florence before joining the Bit Philology project.

Representing born-digital archives in BoDi: towards an application to the forensic layer

Lucia Giagnolini (University of Amsterdam) (online)

In born-digital archives, each document may be considered at once as an inscription on a carrier, as a file managed by software and as a record with its own content and archival context. The Born-Digital Ontology (BoDi), an extension of the Records in Contexts ontology (RiC-O), represents these three layers as distinct entities, together with the file’s metadata and how they were extracted, its hash values and the chain of custody of its carriers.

The archive of Valerio Evangelisti provided an opportunity to test and apply the ontology extension, particularly at the logical and content layers, together with the associated metadata, provenance and integrity information. The forensic layer is now being tested on temporary files recovered from the Gabriel García Márquez archive, an ongoing process that may lead to further developments of BoDi. We present BoDi, the range of archival information it enables us to represent, and the preliminary developments currently being explored on the forensic side.

Lucia Giagnolini is a Lecturer in Archival Science and Digital Humanities at the University of Amsterdam. She holds a PhD in Cultural Heritage in the Digital Ecosystem from the University of Bologna, where she worked as archivist at the University’s Historical Archives. She collaborated with the Centro Manoscritti of the University of Pavia as part of the Pavia Archivi Digitali (PAD) project. She is a member of DH.arc, Digital Humanities Advanced Research Centre, at the University of Bologna and the Amsterdam School for Cultural Analysis. Her research focuses on the transformation of archival practices, with particular attention to the digital context.

Tracing the History of Word Processing in the Archives of Italian Authors

Carmen Ragusa (University of Pavia)

Drawing on materials preserved in the collections of the Centro Manoscritti and the Pavia Archivi Digitali (PAD) project, this talk aims to demonstrate how selected authors’ archives can help reconstruct key stages in the evolution of writing tools and environments since the 1980s. In particular, the papers, digital files and, in some cases, hardware belonging to authors such as Insana, Severgnini and Meneghello offer a snapshot of the gradual democratization of personal computing. These materials document, for example, the emergence and consolidation of IBM-compatible PCs, the spread of different models in the Macintosh family (which helped establish Apple as a key player in shaping the very concept of the personal computer) and the introduction of PowerBook laptops. The talk also highlights how reconstructing the digital contexts (hardware, software and operating systems) in which documents were created provides a valuable interpretative framework for understanding the metadata associated with authors’ files. Ultimately, metadata emerge not merely as technical information, but also as crucial evidence for reconstructing compositional practices in an era of rapid technological change.

Carmen Ragusa is a PhD candidate in Literary and Musical Text Studies at the University of Pavia. Her research develops an integrated approach to the study of authorial digital archives, combining the analysis of textual variants with the investigation of the technological contexts in which digital files were created. She holds a Master’s degree in Italian Studies from the University of Pisa and a second-level University Master’s degree in Textual Computing and Electronic Publishing from the University of Siena. Since November 2025, she has been a member of ALDiNa (opens in a new tab) (Archivi Letterari Digitali Nativi).

On the Genesis of A Montecavallo. Rome as Narcosis: critical approaches to Francesco Pecoraro’s born-digital archive

Anna Guadagnoli (University of Pavia)

Francesco Pecoraro’s short story A Montecavallo. Roma come narcosi was published in the online journal «Snaporaz» in November 2024. The genetic files are scattered throughout the author’s born-digital archive, preserved at the Manuscript Centre of Pavia, rather than being gathered in a single folder dedicated to the literary project. The earliest documents date back to the late 1980s, when Pecoraro began writing on a computer, composing a small collection of verses. The files involved in the genesis of the work make it possible to examine its autobiographical and essayistic dimensions, as well as the influence of the author’s architectural training. Furthermore, the avant-textes offer an opportunity to investigate Francesco Pecoraro’s archive and creative process from a critical perspective, revealing how the author draws on the primordial materials of his writing and reworks them in accordance with the demands of his literary design.

Anna Guadagnoli is a PhD student in Scienze del Testo Letterario e Musicale at the University of Pavia, where she is working on a thesis devoted to the digital archive of Francesco Pecoraro, held at the Centro Manoscritti. She is a member of ALDiNa (opens in a new tab) (Archivi Letterari Digitali Nativi) and is conducting the first studies on Alessandro Spina’s hybrid archive, held at the Archivio Prezzolini in Lugano. Alongside this research, she is also studying Germano Lombardi’s authorial archive, to which she has dedicated an article published in issue 89 of «il verri», entitled «Un concorso nel tempo delle guerre locali di Germano Lombardi. Primi affondi critici di una pièce parzialmente inedita».

Friday 27 November

Two Writers at the Macintosh: Forensic Approaches to the Born-Digital Papers of Gabriel García Márquez and Franco Fortini

Mariangela Giglio (Ca’ Foscari University of Venice, University of Bologna)

In the mid-1980s Gabriel García Márquez and Franco Fortini both began writing on computers, and both chose the Apple Macintosh. Their born-digital legacies, made up of files, drafts and storage media, are now part of our literary heritage. Presenting the two cases side by side, this paper shows how forensic philology, through disk imaging, file-system analysis and the recovery of deleted and overwritten data, turns these digital remains into evidence for the genesis of their texts.

Mariangela Giglio is a doctoral candidate in Cultural Heritage in the Digital Ecosystem at the University of Bologna within the Department of Classical Philology and Italian Studies. She serves as a research fellow for the BLADE project at Ca’ Foscari University of Venice and maintains affiliation with ALDiNa (opens in a new tab) (Archivi Letterari Digitali Nativi). Concentrating on the Gabriel García Márquez Papers at UT Austin’s Harry Ransom Center alongside the Franco Fortini Archive at the University of Siena, her research explores the intersection of born-digital literary archives, forensic philology, and digital humanities.

Recovering the Digital Materials of Maria-Mercè Marçal’s Posthumous Work

Clàudia Pey Asensio (Universitat de Barcelona) (online)

This paper is part of the critical edition project of Maria-Mercè Marçal’s posthumous poetic work and focuses on the recovery and study of the digital witnesses preserved at the Biblioteca de Catalunya.

The author’s archive contains approximately 220 electronic files stored on five floppy disks and created mainly using the WordPerfect word-processing software. Software obsolescence and difficulties in accessing the storage media have prevented the recovery of part of this documentation for many years. Recently, however, it has been possible to recover the file containing the last typescript of the work under the author’s control. This document constitutes a key witness for the ongoing critical edition and has been established as the copy-text against which the manuscript witnesses are collated.

The paper also aims to explore the possibilities offered by the forensic analysis of the original files, particularly in terms of metadata recovery and the retrieval of additional information about the compositional process, while taking into account the preservation conditions of the storage media and the risks involved in reading the floppy disks again.

Clàudia Pey Asensio (Sant Vicenç de Castellet, 1994) is a doctoral researcher with the Contemporary Catalan Literature Research Group in the Department of Catalan Philology and General Linguistics at the University of Barcelona. She holds a BA in Catalan Philology (University of Barcelona) and Master’s degrees in Teacher Training (University of Barcelona) and in Advanced Studies in Catalan Language and Literature (University of Barcelona and Autonomous University of Barcelona). She has worked as a Catalan Studies lecturer for the Institut Ramon Llull at Heidelberg University and the University of Mannheim, as a Catalan language and literature teacher at several secondary schools, and currently as an adjunct lecturer on the Master’s Degree in Teacher Training at the Open University of Catalonia. She has published research on the work of Maria Antònia Salvà, Felícia Fuster and Clementina Arderiu. Since July 2025, she has held a Joan Oró doctoral fellowship and is working on a critical edition of Maria-Mercè Marçal’s posthumous poetic works as part of her doctoral research.

“Save as”: Archival media, born-digital archives and research cultures of loss in Modern Greek Studies

Anna-Maria Sichani (School of Advanced Study, University of London) (online)

For almost a century, Humanities disciplines have been shaped by changing media and technologies. From microfilms to CD-ROMs and web-native databases, scholars and writers have continually adopted new technologies to create, store, circulate and access knowledge. Yet these technologies are far from neutral: they shape not only how knowledge is produced and preserved, but also what disappears or remains inaccessible, creating a ‘negative archive’ of lost, fragmented and forgotten cultural memory. With digital preservation as both a scholarly desideratum and an ongoing professional practice, this presentation explores how the materiality, fragility, obsolescence and loss of archival media and born-digital records reflect and define our research culture. Drawing on case studies from Modern Greek Studies, where engagement with digital preservation and born-digital archives remains limited, it examines archival fragility and loss as ‘always already’ qualities of our (digital) research practice, advocating for a more critical, material- and media-aware approach to scholarship and preservation.

Anna-Maria Sichani is a Senior Researcher at the AHRC-funded Towards a National Collection programme, leading the delivery of National Research Infrastructure for Cultural Heritage prototype (N-RICH), and a Research Associate in Digital Humanities at the School of Advanced Study, University of London. With a background in Digital Humanities, Media History and Modern Greek Studies, she brings more than 8 years of interdisciplinary research, teaching and cross-sectoral work at the intersection of data-intensive approaches and emerging technologies in the Humanities and the wider Cultural Heritage and information environment, critical data studies, critical infrastructure studies and digital pedagogy. Her recent work focuses on digital preservation, fragility and loss in Humanities and cultural heritage digital infrastructures.

Updating the DPC Technology Watch Report - Digital Forensics in Personal Digital Archives

Callum McKean (The British Library), Leontien Talboom (Cambridge University Libraries) (online)

Callum McKean is the Lead Curator for Born Digital Archives and Manuscripts at the British Library, where he has looked after the UK national collection of literary and political personal archives since 2021, overseeing the acquisition, preservation, processing and access provision for these collections at the Library. His research interests include contemporary literature, born-digital manuscripts, and computational approaches to cultural heritage material.

Leontien Talboom is the Technical Analyst at Cambridge University Libraries & Archives, specialising in digital preservation and legacy media. She led the Future Nostalgia project, combining research and expertise to safeguard knowledge about floppy disks. Her work explores the challenges of preserving digital information and ensuring future access.
